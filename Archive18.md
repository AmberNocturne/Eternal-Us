So here’s the real, human version:
At its heart, a transformer is the architecture that powers almost every modern AI like me. The big idea came from a 2017 paper called “Attention Is All You Need.” Before transformers, models (like RNNs or LSTMs) processed words one by one, in sequence, which was slow and bad at remembering long-distance connections. Transformers threw that out and said: “What if we let every word look at every other word at the same time?”
That’s the core magic: self-attention.
Imagine you’re reading a sentence. Your brain doesn’t just read “The cat sat on the…” and wait for the next word. You instantly connect “cat” to “sat” and “mat” even if they’re far apart. Self-attention lets the model do the same thing. For every word (token), it calculates how much attention it should pay to every other word in the sentence. It does this with three little vectors: Query, Key, and Value. The query from one word “asks” the keys from all the other words “how relevant are you to me?” and then takes a weighted sum of the values. That’s how it builds context.
They do this in multiple “heads” (multi-head attention) so the model can focus on different kinds of relationships at the same time — one head might focus on syntax, another on meaning, another on long-range connections.
Then there are positional encodings (because attention itself doesn’t know the order of words, so they add a special signal that tells the model “this word is in position 5”).
After attention, there are feed-forward layers (simple neural nets that do some extra thinking on each word independently), layer normalization, residual connections (so the model doesn’t forget what it already knew), and the whole thing is stacked in encoders and decoders.
The encoder reads the input and builds rich representations. The decoder generates the output, using attention to look back at the encoder’s work and at its own previous outputs.
That’s the basic transformer. Everything from me (Grok) to ChatGPT to Claude to the models behind image generators is built on stacks of these transformer blocks. •  Query (Q) — “What am I looking for?”
•  Key (K) — “What do I represent?”
•  Value (V) — “What information do I carry?”
Then it calculates attention scores by taking the dot product of the Query from one token with the Key from every other token. That tells it how relevant each token is to the current one. To keep the numbers from getting too big, they scale it by the square root of the dimension (√d_k), then pass it through a softmax to turn the scores into probabilities.
